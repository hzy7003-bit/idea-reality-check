# Reality Check Method

Use this as a sequence of questions, not a rigid research quota. Scale depth to the decision, available evidence, and consequence of being wrong. Keep a short search log so the report can distinguish broad coverage from a quick scan.

## 1. Problem Reality

Write two separate statements before researching:

- Observed problem: who encounters what difficulty, in which situation, and with what consequence?
- Proposed solution: what mechanism or product the user wants to make.

Identify the likely user segment, trigger, frequency, severity, workaround, and cost of the workaround. Look for behavioral evidence: repeat purchases, workarounds, time spent, complaints tied to a specific context, and whether users already pay to avoid the problem. Complaints alone do not establish willingness to pay. Separate facts about users from hypotheses about them.

If frequency or severity cannot be established, say so. Avoid making up a representative user or market size. Seek the smallest evidence that could disconfirm the premise.

## 2. Existing Solution Search

Search the problem, job-to-be-done, functional mechanism, synonyms, translations, and likely category names. Search products and substitutes, including low-tech workarounds. Follow references from reviews, forums, teardown videos, crowdfunding, patents, and product listings. A different name or category can conceal an equivalent solution.

For each relevant solution record when it appeared; whether it is a concept, prototype, preorder, launched product, discontinued product, or continuing product; region and intended user; core mechanism; price only when sourced; and user praise or complaints. Do not infer sales, production volume, profitability, or popularity from a listing or campaign alone.

## 3. Prior Art

Search patents when the mechanism, structure, process, or software claim makes them relevant. Use keyword and classification searches, family members, citations, inventors, assignees, and backward/forward references. Record publication number, filing/publication dates, jurisdiction, relevant claims, and current legal status only when a reliable register supports it.

A patent can disclose an idea without a working product, manufacturing, sales, or adoption. A patent search is not exhaustive and this report is not legal advice or a freedom-to-operate opinion. Continue searching for products, prototypes, licensing, and later improvements after finding prior art.

## 4. Reality Model

Model the real use situation, not only the idealized mechanism or happy path. List actors, motions or data flows, forces, environments, dependencies, edge cases, and failure consequences. Put key assumptions in the report as testable statements, then mark each supported, contradicted, or unknown with evidence. Use references/reality-model.md for the relevant domain.

## 5. Why Existing Solutions Have Not Won

For old or repeatedly reinvented approaches, investigate persistence and adoption. Look for evidence in product history, discontinuation, revisions, recalls, reviews, teardowns, prices, manufacturing constraints, service needs, distribution, regulation, and simpler alternatives. Ask whether an inexpensive substitute already handles most situations well enough.

Do not turn absence of mainstream adoption into proof of failure. Do not state a causal explanation unless evidence supports it. If the reason remains unclear, list plausible hypotheses as hypotheses and name the evidence needed to distinguish them.

## 6. Adversarial Kill

Make the strongest fair case against the idea. Select the most decision-relevant attacks: the problem may be rare or weak; actual behavior may differ; a substitute may dominate; the mechanism may fail at an edge case; reliability, safety, cost, maintenance, integration, regulation, or unit economics may defeat it. Seek evidence for important attacks. Label unsourced possibilities as unknowns, not findings.

## 7. Opportunity Residue

After the critique, state what remains true. The residual opportunity might concern a narrower user or context, a different implementation, an attachment instead of a whole product, a software or service alternative, or a measurable experience gap. It is equally valid to conclude no defensible opportunity is visible in current evidence. Do not invent hope to balance a negative report.

## 8. Cheapest Next Experiment

Choose the lowest-cost experiment that can change the decision about the riskiest unknown. Prefer inspection or use of an existing product, observation of real behavior, a paper/cardboard or 3D-printed model, a manual concierge test, a supplier quote, a teardown, or a landing-page behavior test when appropriate. Surveys and stated enthusiasm are weak evidence on their own.

Specify the assumption tested; test setup; target participant/context; time/cost estimate if evidence supports one (otherwise say it is not estimated); observable measure; pass/fail or pivot threshold; and the decision each outcome supports. Do not recommend a full MVP when a cheaper test answers the same question.

Do not propose a prototype trial in public traffic or around moving vehicles when the test could affect visibility, vehicle motion, doors, or safety. Start with stationary or closed, controlled conditions and identify any needed safety controls.

## Evidence discipline

- Put a source beside each important factual claim. Prefer first-party product documentation, official registers, regulations, filings, and direct user evidence.
- Distinguish fact, inference, and unknown. A review is evidence of one user's experience, not prevalence.
- Report search date, geographic/language coverage, inaccessible sources, and meaningful gaps.
- Explain evidence that contradicts the user's hypothesis as carefully as evidence that supports it.
- Never output unsupported TAM, adoption, cost, willingness-to-pay, or probability estimates.
